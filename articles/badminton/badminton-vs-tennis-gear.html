Badminton vs Tennis Gear

Updated August 2026 ֲ· 6 min read ֲ· Affiliate links may earn us a commission

Home / Badminton / Badminton vs Tennis Gear

Plenty of people arrive at badminton from tennis, reasonably assuming that a racket sport is a racket sport. Some of the kit does transfer. The rackets absolutely do not, and the reasons are more interesting than they look.

The Rackets Are Not Comparable

A tennis racket weighs around 300 grams strung and is strung at 50 to 60 lbs. A badminton racket weighs around 85 grams and is strung at 20 to 24 lbs. That is roughly a third of the weight at roughly a third of the tension — not a variation on the same object, a different category of tool.

The reason is the projectile. A tennis ball weighs 58 grams and arrives with momentum you have to redirect, so the racket needs mass to avoid being pushed around. A shuttlecock weighs 5 grams and stops almost immediately, so the racket needs speed, not mass — and every gram you remove is a gram your arm does not have to accelerate.

The practical consequence for a tennis player is that badminton strokes are wrist and forearm rotation, not shoulder and body rotation. A tennis swing applied to a badminton racket produces a shot with plenty of arm effort and very little shuttle speed.

What Does Transfer

Overgrips — completely interchangeable

This is the one item where the products are literally the same. Tennis overgrips, badminton overgrips and squash overgrips are identical tape sold in different packaging, and several of the best-value multi-packs are marketed for tennis. Buy whichever is cheaper.

Bags — near-interchangeable

A badminton racket is slightly longer than a tennis racket and much lighter, so it fits any tennis bag comfortably. The only genuine difference is that badminton-specific bags have narrower racket sleeves, which stops light frames sliding around in a compartment sized for heavier ones. Not a reason to buy twice.

Fitness — mostly, with a caveat

Court movement, split-step timing and the general ability to change direction all carry over well. What does not is the movement pattern itself: tennis is largely lateral along a baseline, badminton is a repeated lunge to four corners with a lot of overhead work. Tennis players usually find their legs are fine and their shoulders and calves are not.

What Does Not Transfer

Shoes — and this one matters

Tennis shoes are built for outdoor hard courts or clay: a durable outsole, a herringbone tread designed for a gritty surface, and a relatively tall midsole. Badminton shoes have a flat gum-rubber sole for grip on sprung wood, a much lower profile so the ankle sits closer to the floor, and reinforcement for the deep forward lunge that badminton demands constantly.

Wearing tennis shoes indoors is also often prohibited outright, because their outsoles mark wooden floors. A dedicated indoor court shoe is the one purchase that does not carry over and should not be skipped.

Strings — not even slightly

Tennis string is far thicker and is strung at two to three times the tension. Beyond being physically wrong, a badminton frame will not survive tennis tension — the frames are built for around 24 lbs and stringing higher deforms or cracks them. Badminton frames also need a badminton stringing machine, because tennis clamps will crush them.

Technique — the big one

Tennis rewards a long, accelerating swing driven from the body. Badminton rewards a short backswing and an explosive forearm rotation at the last moment — the racket head does most of its accelerating in the final few inches. Tennis players commonly arrive with more raw athleticism than the people beating them and lose anyway, because the shuttle is gone before their swing has finished.

The Short Version

Bring your overgrips, your bag and your fitness. Buy a badminton racket, badminton string and indoor court shoes — none of those transfer, and the shoes are a genuine injury issue rather than a performance one. Expect the first month to be humbling: the equipment is easy to replace, the swing is not.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use a tennis racket for badminton?

No, in any practical sense. A tennis racket weighs around three times as much and cannot be swung fast enough to move a 5-gram shuttlecock properly, and the string bed is far too tight to give any feel. You would also be exhausted in ten minutes. The two rackets look related and are engineered around completely different problems.

Are badminton and tennis overgrips the same?

Yes, genuinely the same product. Overgrip tape for tennis, badminton, squash and pickleball is identical and sold in different packaging, and some of the best-value multi-packs are marketed for tennis. Buy whichever is cheaper per grip — there is no badminton-specific property to look for.

Can I wear tennis shoes for badminton?

Usually not, and you should not want to. Tennis shoes are built for outdoor courts with tall midsoles and hard outsoles that mark wooden floors — many sports halls ban them for that reason alone. Badminton shoes are flat, low to the ground and gum-soled for grip on sprung wood, with lateral and forward-lunge reinforcement. This is the one item where the difference is an injury risk.

Is badminton easier than tennis if I already play tennis?

The basic rally is easier to start, and the competitive game is harder than most tennis players expect. Your court movement and hand-eye coordination transfer well. Your swing does not — badminton power comes from a short backswing and explosive forearm rotation, while tennis power comes from a long swing driven by the body. Unlearning that takes longer than the fitness does.

Why is badminton string tension so much lower than tennis?

Because the frames and the projectile are different. A badminton frame weighs about 85 grams and is built to withstand roughly 24 lbs; tennis frames are three times heavier and take 50 to 60 lbs. A shuttlecock also weighs a tenth of a tennis ball, so it does not need a tight bed to be redirected — it needs a bed that deforms and throws it back.
