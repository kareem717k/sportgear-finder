Best Badminton Sets for Families (2026)

Badminton is the best garden sport there is — it needs no skill to start, no space to speak of, and a shuttlecock does not break windows. The problem is that "badminton set" describes two completely different products, and buying the wrong one is why so many garden sets end up in the shed after one summer.

Work out which of the two you need first, and the rest of the decision is straightforward.

Racket Sets and Garden Kits Are Not the Same Thing

A racket set is two or four matched rackets, some shuttles and a bag. No net. A garden kit is a net and posts plus, usually, four cheap rackets. They cost roughly the same and they are not competing with each other.

If you already have somewhere to play — a club, a park with a net, a beach — buy a racket set, because all the money goes into the rackets. If you need a net in the garden, you have a choice: buy a garden kit and accept mediocre rackets, or buy a good net and a good racket set separately for maybe forty dollars more. The second option is much better equipment and it is what we would do.

How Many Rackets

This is the decision that determines quality more than the price does. Spending $50 on four rackets means each frame cost twelve dollars; spending $50 on two means each cost twenty-five. Twelve-dollar rackets are aluminium heads on steel shafts and feel dead. Twenty-five-dollar rackets can be graphite.

So buy the four-racket set only if four people genuinely play at once. If it is usually two of you with occasional guests, buy the two-racket set and add a cheap pair later.

Aluminium or Graphite

Sets under about $45 are almost all aluminium head on a steel shaft. That construction is heavy and flexes in the middle of the shaft rather than whipping through, so shots feel mushy — but it is also very hard to break, which is genuinely the right property for equipment that lives in a garage and gets lent to visitors.

Graphite sets start around $55 and feel like a different sport. Buy aluminium if this is a social thing that happens four times a summer. Buy graphite if anyone in the house is going to start caring.

If You Need a Net Too

The nets bundled with garden kits are usually fine for casual play, but they share one failure: the poles bow inwards under the net's tension and the middle sags within twenty minutes. Standalone nets fix that with thicker poles, guy ropes, or a tensioning winch.

Height matters more than people expect. Regulation is 5ft 1in at the posts, and adults who have played before will notice immediately if a net is short. Children, on the other hand, have far better rallies with the net lowered — which is why height-adjustable nets are the right answer for a mixed-age garden.

What to Upgrade First

In order: the shuttles, the grips, then the rackets. Bundled shuttles are the cheapest hard nylon on the market and they fly flat and dead — a $10 tube of proper nylon changes the game more than any other ten dollars you could spend.

Then overgrips, at a dollar or two each, because every set racket ships with a moulded plastic handle that gets slippery the moment hands warm up. Only after both of those is the racket itself the thing holding anybody back.

The Short Version

Two people playing: buy the Yonex GR 303 Combo. Four people playing regularly: buy the Senston four-racket carbon set. Two people who are getting serious: buy the Senston N80 pair. Buy the net separately rather than taking the one bundled into a cheap kit, and spend ten dollars on real shuttles whatever you decide.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a badminton set better value than buying rackets separately?

For two or more players, usually yes — a matched pair costs about 20 to 30 percent less than the same rackets bought individually, with covers and a bag included. For one player it is worse value, because half the money goes into a racket that sits in a cupboard. The exception is club players who deliberately carry a spare for broken strings.

Do badminton sets include a net?

Some do, most two-player and four-player racket sets do not. Racket sets normally include rackets, shuttles and a bag. Full garden kits include a net and posts, but usually spend so little on the rackets that they are barely playable. Buying a good racket set plus a separate net gives much better equipment for similar money.

What size badminton net do I need for a garden?

Regulation is 20ft wide and 5ft 1in high at the posts, dipping to 5ft in the centre. Most portable garden nets are 10 to 15ft wide, which means the doubles tramlines do not exist — fine for casual play, wrong if anyone is practising. If space allows, buy full width; if children are playing, buy height-adjustable and drop the net.

Are the rackets in a family badminton set good enough for adults?

For social play, yes. They are normally full adult length, so the issue is the opposite of what people expect — children under about eight find them long and heavy. The rackets will feel underpowered and mushy to any adult who has played properly, because aluminium-and-steel construction flexes in the wrong place. That is the price of four rackets in one box.

What should I upgrade first on a cheap badminton set?

The shuttlecocks, then the grips, then the rackets. A $10 tube of proper nylon shuttles changes how the game plays immediately — the ones bundled with sets are the cheapest hard nylon available. A $2 overgrip fixes the slippery moulded handle. Only after both is a racket upgrade what is holding you back.
