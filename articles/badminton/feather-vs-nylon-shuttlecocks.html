Feather vs Nylon Shuttlecocks

Updated August 2026 ֲ· 7 min read ֲ· Affiliate links may earn us a commission

Home / Badminton / Feather vs Nylon Shuttlecocks

This is the only genuine fork in badminton equipment, and it is not a quality question. Feather shuttles are not the good ones and nylon the cheap ones — they fly differently, cost differently, and are correct in different places.

The short answer: nylon for practice and anywhere outdoors, feather for indoor matches. Here is why, and what it actually costs.

The Flight Is Genuinely Different

A feather shuttle is sixteen goose or duck feathers glued into a cork base. The skirt is porous and slightly irregular, which creates enormous drag the moment the shuttle slows down. So a feather shuttle leaves the racket fast and then brakes hard — that steep, almost vertical drop at the end of its flight is what makes badminton's drop shots and net play work as a game.

A nylon shuttle has a moulded one-piece skirt. It is smoother, more rigid, and produces less drag at low speed, so it carries further and flattens out instead of dropping. The practical effect is that shots which should die over the net instead land at the service line, and clears that should sit down instead sail long.

None of this makes nylon bad. It makes it a different flight model, and one that happens to be much better suited to wind, concrete and beginners.

Durability Is the Real Trade

A feather shuttle can be finished in a single rally. Feathers split when they are hit by a frame rather than strings, and a split feather changes the flight immediately even though the shuttle looks intact. Club players at a decent standard typically budget roughly one shuttle per game.

A nylon shuttle survives being mis-hit, trodden on, played on grass and left in a bag for six months. Recreational players get weeks or months out of one, and it usually dies from a cracked skirt or a squashed cork rather than from wear.

Put those together and the cost per hour is not close. A $23 tube of twelve feather shuttles might cover two or three club evenings. A $16 tube of six Mavis nylon shuttles covers a season of the same play.

Speed Ratings Are About the Room, Not About You

Both types are sold as slow, medium or fast, and this confuses nearly everyone the first time. It is not a difficulty setting and it is not about how hard you hit. It is a correction for air density.

Cold, thin, high-altitude air produces less drag, so a shuttle carries further and you need a slower one. Hot, humid, sea-level air produces more drag, so you need a faster one. The standard test is a full-power underarm clear from the back tramline: the shuttle should land between the back doubles service line and the baseline.

For a heated indoor hall at ordinary altitude, medium or slow is right. If you are guessing, buy medium.

What to Buy for Where You Play

Garden, driveway or park

Nylon, and the heavier outdoor variety if there is any breeze at all. Feather shuttles in wind are simply unplayable — they weigh under 5 grams and a light gust moves them a metre. This is not a compromise; it is the only workable option.

Storing Feather Shuttles

Feathers are organic and they dry out. A tube left in a hot car or a dry heated room for a month produces shuttles that shatter on the first clean smash, and people almost always blame the manufacturer for this.

The standard fix is humidity: keep the tube sealed, store it somewhere cool, and before a session put a slightly damp cloth or sponge in the tube for a few hours. Rehydrated feathers are flexible again and survive normally. Nylon needs none of this, which is another quiet argument for training on it.

The Short Version

If you play outdoors or you are still learning, buy nylon and buy the good nylon rather than the cheapest — the flight consistency is what you are paying for. If you play indoor club matches, buy feather and accept it as a running cost of roughly one shuttle per game. Buy one tube of feather regardless, just so you know what the sport is supposed to feel like.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are feather shuttlecocks better than nylon?

They fly the way the sport was designed around — braking hard at the end of the flight, which is what makes drop shots and net play work. But they are destroyed quickly, cost far more per hour, and are unusable outdoors or in any wind. Better is the wrong frame: feather is correct for indoor matches, nylon is correct for practice and outdoors.

How long does a feather shuttlecock last?

Anywhere from a single rally to a couple of games. Feathers split when hit by the frame rather than the strings, and a split feather changes the flight immediately even if the shuttle looks fine. Club players at a decent standard typically budget about one shuttle per game, which is why clubs pool the cost rather than individuals buying them.

Why do my feather shuttlecocks break so quickly?

Usually because they are too dry rather than defective. Feathers are organic and lose flexibility in dry heated air, then shatter on the first solid hit. Store tubes sealed and cool, and put a damp sponge or cloth in the tube for a few hours before a session. Mis-hits off the frame are the other cause, and that one only technique fixes.

What speed shuttlecock should I buy?

Medium, unless you know your hall is unusually hot or cold. Speed corrects for air density, not skill — cold thin air needs a faster shuttle, hot humid air needs a slower one. Test with a full underarm clear from the back tramline: it should land between the back doubles service line and the baseline.

Can I use nylon shuttlecocks for competitive badminton?

For club and league play, often yes — many leagues specify nylon precisely to control costs, and Yonex Mavis shuttles are the usual standard. Sanctioned tournament badminton is played on feather. If you are training for feather competition, practise on feather at least some of the time, because the timing does not transfer perfectly.
