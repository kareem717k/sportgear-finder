Pickleball Paddle Weight & Core Explained

Updated August 2026 ֲ· 5 min read ֲ· Affiliate links may earn us a commission

Home / Pickleball / Pickleball Paddle Weight & Core Explained

Two numbers on a paddle spec sheet change how it plays more than anything else: weight and core thickness. Almost everything else — face material, shape, handle length — is a refinement on top of those. Get these two right and you will like almost any paddle. Get them wrong and no amount of carbon will save it.

Core Thickness: Control vs Pop

The core is the honeycomb layer sandwiched between the two faces. Thickness is usually 13mm or 16mm, and the difference is genuinely large.

  • 16mm — more flex, longer dwell time, softer response. Easier to control on dinks, drops and resets, and far more forgiving on off-centre contact. The default recommendation for anyone still developing a soft game.
  • 13mm — firmer, faster off the face, more raw pop. Rewards players who can already redirect pace at the net. Less forgiving when you miss the middle.

The common mistake is assuming thinner means more advanced. It does not — plenty of very strong players use 16mm precisely because the control matters more than the extra pop. What thinner really means is less margin for error.

Weight: Stability vs Hand Speed

Most paddles land between 7.3 and 8.5 ounces. That sounds like a trivial range, and it is not — an ounce is very noticeable in a fast hands battle at the kitchen.

  • Under 7.8 oz (light) — fastest hands, easiest to manoeuvre, least strain on the arm. Gets pushed around by hard drives and gives you less free power.
  • 7.8 to 8.3 oz (mid) — where most players should be. Enough mass to hold up against pace without slowing your hands noticeably.
  • Over 8.3 oz (heavy) — most stable and most free power on drives and overheads. Slower at the net and harder on the elbow over a long session.

Swing Weight Is the Number Nobody Prints

Two paddles can weigh the same on a scale and feel completely different, because it matters where the weight sits. An elongated paddle carries more of its mass away from your hand, so it feels heavier through the swing even at identical static weight. This is why an 8.0 oz elongated paddle can feel more tiring than an 8.2 oz standard one. If a paddle feels sluggish despite a reasonable weight, swing weight is usually why.

How to Choose Without Guessing

Start with core thickness: 16mm unless you specifically want a fast, punchy paddle for net exchanges. Then pick weight by what goes wrong in your game — if you get overpowered, go heavier; if you are late in hands battles, go lighter. Only then worry about shape, and remember that elongated adds reach at the cost of forgiveness and swing weight.

You Can Add Weight, But Not Remove It

Lead tape on the edge guard is a cheap and reversible way to add mass, and where you put it changes the feel: tape at 3 and 9 o'clock adds stability across the face, tape at 12 o'clock adds power and swing weight. What you cannot do is make a heavy paddle lighter. If you are unsure, buy at the lighter end of your range and add — it is the only direction that works.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 13mm or 16mm better for pickleball?

Neither is objectively better. 16mm gives more control, more dwell time and a bigger effective sweet spot, which suits most recreational and improving players. 13mm is faster off the face and better for players who counter-attack hard at the net. Most people should start at 16mm.

What weight pickleball paddle should I use?

Between 7.8 and 8.3 ounces suits the majority of players. Go lighter if you are frequently late in fast net exchanges or have any elbow trouble; go heavier if hard drives push your paddle around and you want more free power.

Does a heavier paddle cause tennis elbow?

It contributes. Heavier paddles transmit more shock and demand more from the forearm, and higher swing weight makes it worse. But the more common causes are a grip that is too small, a worn-out overgrip that makes you squeeze, and a paddle that is too stiff. Fix the grip before blaming the weight.

What is swing weight and why does it matter?

Swing weight measures how heavy a paddle feels while you move it, not how heavy it is on a scale. Mass positioned further from your hand raises it. Two paddles at identical static weight can feel very different because of it, which is why elongated shapes feel more tiring than standard ones.

Can I add weight to a pickleball paddle?

Yes — lead or tungsten tape on the edge guard is standard practice and fully reversible. Tape at 3 and 9 o'clock adds stability, tape at 12 o'clock adds power. You cannot make a paddle lighter, so buy light and add if you are unsure.
