Best Pickleball Paddles for Beginners (2026)

Updated July 2026 ֲ· 6 min read ֲ· Affiliate links may earn us a commission

Buying a first pickleball paddle is harder than it should be. The same brand will sell you something at $40 and something at $250, and the marketing on both talks about carbon and cores without ever saying who should buy which. Here is the short answer: a beginner wants a 16mm core, a standard shape, and to spend well under $100. Everything below explains why, and what happens if you ignore that.

Skip the Wooden Paddles Entirely

Almost everyone's first experience of pickleball is with a wooden paddle from a cheap net set. They are heavy — often 10 or 11 ounces against a normal 8 — dead on contact, and they teach you to muscle the ball because nothing comes off the face on its own. Playing with one for a session is fine. Buying one is not. A basic composite paddle costs very little more and is a completely different object.

Core Thickness Is the Only Spec That Really Matters at First

Paddle cores come in roughly two thicknesses, and this single number changes more about how a paddle plays than the brand on the face.

  • 16mm cores flex more on contact. The ball stays on the face fractionally longer, which slows it down and makes dinks, drops and resets far easier to control. Off-centre hits still go where you intended.
  • 13mm cores are firmer and punchier. The ball leaves faster, which is great for counter-attacking at the net if you already have the hands for it, and unhelpful if you do not.

If you are below a 4.0 rating, buy 16mm. The forgiveness shows up on every single mis-hit, and mis-hits are most of what a beginner does.

Face Material: What You Are Actually Paying For

Fibreglass faces are cheapest and give the most raw pop, which flatters a beginner's drives but makes soft shots harder to control. Graphite is calmer and more predictable — easier to place the ball. Raw carbon fibre has a gritty surface that holds the ball long enough to generate real spin, which is why every premium paddle now uses it. But raw carbon only pays off once you can deliberately brush up the back of the ball. Before that, you are paying for a feature you cannot yet use.

Best Overall for Beginners

Onix Graphite MOD Z5

The default step-up recommendation for years and still the right answer for most people. The widebody shape gives one of the most forgiving sweet spots available, so shots struck away from centre still make it over the net with something on them. The Nomex core is firmer and louder than polymer, which means you get clear feedback about where on the face you actually hit it — genuinely useful while you are learning to control depth. It will not generate much spin, but nothing you should be buying at this stage will.

The Short Version

Buy the ONIX Graphite Z5 if you want the most forgiving paddle for the money. Buy the Niupipo if you are still deciding whether pickleball is for you. Buy the Selkirk SLK Evo if you already know you are staying and want a 16mm core you will not outgrow in a season. Do not buy a $200 carbon paddle as your first paddle — it will punish you for exactly the mistakes beginners make.

What About Grip Size?

Most paddles ship with a grip between 4.125 and 4.25 inches, which suits the majority of adult hands. The rule worth remembering is that you can always make a grip bigger by adding an overgrip, and it is very difficult to make one smaller. If you are between sizes, choose the smaller paddle and build it up. A grip that is too large forces you to squeeze harder, which is a direct route to elbow pain.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should a beginner spend on a pickleball paddle?

Somewhere between $40 and $100. Below $40 you are usually buying a wooden paddle or a very poor composite one. Above $100 you are paying for spin and shape characteristics that only help once your technique is repeatable. Most beginners are best served around the $70 to $90 mark.

Is a heavier or lighter pickleball paddle better for beginners?

Most beginners do best between 7.8 and 8.3 ounces. Lighter paddles are quicker to manoeuvre at the net but get pushed around by pace. Heavier paddles are more stable and generate more power with less effort, but slow your hands down in fast exchanges and can aggravate the elbow.

Do I need a USAPA approved paddle?

Only for sanctioned tournament play. For social and league play nobody checks. That said, essentially every paddle worth buying carries the approval anyway, so there is no reason to deliberately choose one that does not.

How long before I should upgrade my paddle?

Not until your shots are repeatable enough that you can feel what a paddle is doing. For most people that is six months to a year of regular play. Upgrading earlier tends to mean buying a paddle that demands cleaner contact than you can reliably produce.

Can I use a table tennis or tennis racket for pickleball?

No. A table tennis bat is far too small and a tennis racket is strung and much too large — neither is legal or playable. Pickleball paddles are a specific size and solid-faced, and there is no substitute.
