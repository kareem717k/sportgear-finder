Indoor vs Outdoor Pickleballs

Updated August 2026 ֲ· 4 min read ֲ· Affiliate links may earn us a commission

Home / Pickleball / Indoor vs Outdoor Pickleballs

This is the single most common piece of confusion in pickleball, and unlike most gear debates it has a clear answer. Indoor and outdoor pickleballs are not interchangeable. They differ in hole count, wall thickness, weight and plastic formulation, and playing with the wrong one changes the game noticeably.

The Actual Differences

  • Outdoor balls have 40 small holes, thicker walls and more mass. The extra weight means wind pushes them around less, and the harder plastic makes them fly faster and hit harder.
  • Indoor balls have around 26 larger holes, thinner walls and softer plastic. They travel slower, are quieter, and take spin more readily because the softer surface grips the paddle face.

The practical result: an outdoor ball on a smooth gym floor skids, comes off the paddle faster than you expect and stings the hands. An indoor ball outdoors gets blown off line by any breeze and cracks quickly in cold weather. Neither is a small difference.

Why Outdoor Balls Crack So Often

Every pickleball is a single piece of moulded plastic being hit hard, repeatedly, often on abrasive concrete. Cracking is not a defect, it is the end of the ball's life. Cold makes it dramatically worse because the plastic becomes brittle — a ball that would last weeks in summer can split on a cold morning. If you play outdoors through winter, buy a ball specifically formulated to stay flexible at low temperatures.

The Short Version

Buy for the surface you actually play on, and do not try to make one ball do both jobs. Outdoors, the Franklin X-40 is the default and the ONIX Fuse G2 is the cold-weather answer. Indoors, the ONIX Fuse Indoor is the safe pick. If your club or league supplies balls, use theirs — consistency across a session matters more than brand preference.

When to Bin a Ball

As soon as it cracks, and before that if you can squeeze it noticeably out of round. A cracked ball does not just feel softer — it flies unpredictably, which quietly teaches you to compensate for something that is not your technique. Balls are consumable and cheap relative to everything else in the sport. Keeping a bad one in play is a false economy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use an outdoor pickleball indoors?

You can, but it plays badly. The heavier, harder outdoor ball skids on a smooth gym floor, comes off the paddle faster than expected and stings the hands. Most indoor venues have standardised on an indoor ball for exactly this reason.

How many holes does an outdoor pickleball have?

40 small holes, against roughly 26 larger ones on an indoor ball. The smaller holes and thicker walls are what make outdoor balls heavier and more wind-resistant.

How long does an outdoor pickleball last?

Anywhere from a few sessions to a single cold morning. Abrasive courts and low temperatures are what kill them. Indoor balls last considerably longer because both the surface and the temperature are gentler.

Are more expensive pickleballs actually better?

Somewhat. Pricier balls tend to be more consistent between units and hold their shape longer, which matters in matches. But no ball is crack-proof, and the gap between a mid-priced and a premium ball is much smaller than the gap between using the right ball type and the wrong one.

What colour pickleball is easiest to see?

Whatever contrasts most with your court and background. Yellow and neon green are the safest outdoor defaults; on a dark indoor floor a lighter ball tracks better. Colour has no effect on flight, only on visibility.
