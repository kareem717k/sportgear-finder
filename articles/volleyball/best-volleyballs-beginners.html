Buying your first volleyball is more confusing than it needs to be. The same brand sells balls at $28 and $85, and the marketing on each one says very little about who should actually buy it. Here's the honest answer: most beginners need a $35–45 composite indoor ball, and the details below explain exactly why.

Indoor vs Beach: Pick One First

This is the most important decision. Indoor and beach volleyballs are not interchangeable:

  • Indoor volleyballs are slightly smaller and heavier, with a leather or composite microfibre cover designed for fast, predictable play on hardwood. They're built for 18 players across a net in an enclosed gym.
  • Beach volleyballs are slightly larger and lighter, with a water-resistant cover and softer feel designed for sand, wind, and bright sunlight. They're built for 4 players in the open air.

Using an indoor ball on sand ruins the cover. Using a beach ball indoors feels dead and unpredictable. Buy for the surface you actually play on.

What to Avoid

  • Rubber volleyballs under $20: The cover is too hard, the sting on forearm passes discourages beginners, and the round shape degrades quickly. The price difference between rubber and composite is $15–20. Always buy composite.
  • FIVB match balls as your first ball: The Mikasa V200W and Molten equivalents are excellent, but at $75–85 they're more ball than a beginner needs. Buy a practice composite and upgrade when you're playing regularly.
  • Balls without a stated approval level: Generic no-brand balls on Amazon often have rubber cores and cheap covers dressed up in composite-looking panels. If the listing doesn't say NFHS, USAV, or FIVB, assume it's rubber.

FAQs

What size volleyball should a beginner buy?

Adult volleyball is size 5 — this is the standard for all adult play. Youth players (typically under 12) use a size 4 or lightweight ball to reduce the sting on passes and make service easier. If you're buying for an adult beginner, buy size 5.

What pressure should I inflate it to?

Almost every volleyball inflates to 4.3–4.6 psi for indoor play. The exact figure is printed next to the valve on the ball. Over-inflation makes the ball hard and painful on forearm passes; under-inflation makes it feel dead. Use a gauge — hand pressure alone isn't accurate enough. Check monthly, as balls lose pressure gradually.

Can I use an indoor volleyball outside?

Occasionally, yes. Regularly, no. The composite leather cover degrades on rough outdoor surfaces and in wet conditions. If you're playing outside on concrete or grass regularly, buy a ball with a durable outdoor-rated cover or an old indoor ball you don't mind retiring early.
