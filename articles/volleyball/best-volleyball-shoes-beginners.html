Best Volleyball Shoes for Beginners (2026)

Updated July 2026 · 6 min read · Affiliate links may earn us a commission

Running shoes feel fine on a volleyball court — until you cut hard, jump, or slide for a dig and realize the grip and cushioning are completely wrong for the movement. Volleyball shoes are built around one surface (indoor hardwood or sport court) and one pattern of stress (repeated lateral cuts, high-jump landings, and quick direction changes). Here are the three we'd actually recommend to someone just starting out.

What Makes a Volleyball Shoe Different?

Three things separate a volleyball shoe from a general trainer:

  • Gum rubber outsole: The soft, slightly sticky rubber used on indoor court shoes grips sport court and hardwood without marking the floor. Running shoe rubber is too hard and slides on polished surfaces.
  • Low heel-to-toe drop: Volleyball shoes keep you close to the ground for lateral stability. A high-drop running shoe shifts your weight backward, which is wrong for every volleyball movement.
  • Forefoot cushioning: You land on the ball of your foot after every jump. Volleyball shoes front-load cushioning where you actually need it — running shoes cushion the heel for forward motion.

For beginners: Don't overthink it. A dedicated volleyball shoe from ASICS, Mizuno, or Nike at any budget is a significant upgrade over a running shoe. The differences are real and immediate.

Best Overall for Beginners

ASICS Upcourt 5

The most-recommended beginner volleyball shoe on the market, and for good reason. The Upcourt 5 has a proper gum rubber outsole that grips indoor courts well, a low profile that keeps you close to the ground on cuts, and ASICS's Trusstic system that stops the midfoot from twisting on lateral movements. The cushioning is modest — this is a court shoe, not a plush trainer — but it handles the repetitive jump landings of a beginner's practice without issue. Available in a wide range of sizes including wide fits, and under $70 in most colourways. The standard starting point that most coaches will recommend.

Best Step-Up

Mizuno Wave Lightning Z8

If your budget stretches to around $140, the Wave Lightning Z8 is a meaningful step up. Mizuno's Wave plate — a rippled sheet between the foam layers — spreads landing force sideways instead of straight up the leg, which you notice on long sessions where the cumulative impact of jumps adds up. The fit is snug and precise; Mizuno runs a touch narrow, so if you're between sizes or have wide feet, take the wide version or try the ASICS first. Outsole grip is excellent on any clean indoor surface. This is a shoe that carries you from recreational play into competitive club, so it won't need replacing as you improve.

Best for Quick Defenders

Nike Zoom HyperSpeed Court

Nike's dedicated volleyball shoe sits around $85 and is the lightest option here by some margin. A Zoom Air unit under the forefoot gives a snappy, responsive feel rather than a soft one, and the ankle straps built into the lacing pull the collar tight so nothing shifts when you change direction on a dig. What to Avoid as a Beginner

  • Running shoes: Wrong heel drop, wrong grip rubber, wrong cushioning location. You'll notice the difference on the first lateral cut.
  • Basketball shoes: Too heavy and too much ankle restriction. Volleyball requires faster foot movements than basketball.
  • Cross-trainers: Slightly better than running shoes but still not built for the repeated jump-land cycle. Fine in a pinch, not ideal.
  • High-top volleyball shoes: Unless prescribed for an ankle injury, low or mid-tops allow the natural ankle movement volleyball requires. High-tops restrict the lateral ankle flex you need for quick cuts.

FAQs

Can I use basketball shoes for volleyball?

Technically yes, but they're heavier, more restrictive, and the rubber compound isn't optimised for the continuous lateral cutting and jumping of volleyball. If you already own quality basketball shoes and are just trying the sport, they'll do — but a dedicated volleyball shoe is noticeably better for sustained play.

How long do volleyball shoes last?

With 2–3 sessions per week, a quality pair lasts 12–18 months before the outsole grip degrades and the cushioning compresses. The gum rubber outsole is usually the first to go — when it no longer feels sticky on court, it's time to replace. Store them in a bag away from sunlight to extend the grip life.

Do I need ankle support in a volleyball shoe?

The shoe itself provides minimal ankle support — that's by design, since you need ankle flexibility for lateral cuts. Ankle support comes from braces and proper conditioning. If you have a history of ankle sprains, pair any volleyball shoe with a lace-up ankle brace rather than relying on a high-top shoe. See our ankle braces guide.
