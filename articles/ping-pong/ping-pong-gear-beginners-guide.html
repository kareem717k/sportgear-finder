Ping Pong Gear: The Complete Beginner's Guide

Updated August 2026 ֲ· 8 min read ֲ· Affiliate links may earn us a commission

Home / Ping Pong / Ping Pong Gear: The Complete Beginner's Guide

Ping pong has one of the lowest equipment barriers in sport, and one of the most confusing shopping experiences, because the same three words — paddle, ball, net — cover items ranging from $8 to $400. This guide sets out what you actually need, in the order it is worth buying, and what you can ignore.

The headline: you do not need a ping pong table. That single fact changes the maths for most people, and it is the thing the sport is worst at communicating.

You Do Not Need a Table

A regulation table is 9 feet by 5 feet and needs about 19 feet of room length to play on properly. Most people do not have that, conclude that ping pong is not for them, and stop.

The alternative is a retractable net that clamps onto whatever table you already own — a dining table, a kitchen island, a desk, a conference table. It extends to the width of the surface, locks, and comes off in seconds. The court is smaller than regulation, so the game becomes faster and more about hands than footwork, but it is unquestionably ping pong and it costs about $25.

If you have a garage or basement and genuinely want a table, buy one. If you do not, a net is not a compromise you settle for — it is how a large share of casual players actually play.

What to Buy, In Order

Spend in this sequence and each purchase improves the next one.

  • 1. A net (~$25) if you have no table. Nothing else matters until there is somewhere to play.
  • 2. Paddles ($40 each, or a four-paddle set for around $28) depending on whether you are equipping yourself or a household.
  • 3. Balls ($14 for a box of 3-star, or ~$18 for a hundred practice balls). Buy more than feels sensible.
  • 4. Nothing, for a while. Play for two months before buying anything else. What to upgrade will become obvious.
  • 5. Optional: a catch net or ball collector (~$20 to $45) if you start practising rather than just playing.

Paddles: One Good One or Four Cheap Ones

This is genuinely a fork in the road, and the right answer depends entirely on who is playing.

If you are the player and others are occasional guests, buy one $40 to $50 paddle for yourself and a cheap set for everyone else. One decent paddle will do more for your enjoyment than four mediocre ones.

If you are equipping a household, a common room or a holiday house, buy a four-paddle set. Four set paddles typically cost less than one mid-range individual paddle, and the failure mode of a household — someone always waiting for a bat — is worse than the failure mode of a slightly ordinary paddle.

The one thing not to compromise on either way is the rubber. Sponge-backed inverted rubber grips the ball and lets you spin it; the hard slick sheet on bargain paddles does not, and a game without spin gets boring fast. Our beginner paddle guide goes into which is which.

Balls: Buy Far More Than You Think

Ping pong balls are 40+ millimetre plastic, weigh 2.7 grams, and are graded from one to three stars. The stars measure consistency — roundness, weight distribution and bounce — not durability. Three-star balls bounce identically to each other, which matters in a match because it means anything odd the ball does was spin, not the ball.

Balls do not really wear out; they crack, almost always because someone stood on one. Assume attrition. A box of six will vanish faster than you expect, and a hundred practice balls costs about what two coffees do. Orange and white play identically — pick whichever contrasts with your floor.

What You Can Skip Entirely

A few things are marketed hard at beginners and are not worth early money.

  • Ping pong shoes. Real, and genuinely useful at club level for lateral grip. Completely unnecessary until you are moving your feet enough to notice.
  • Rubber cleaner and protective film. A damp cloth does most of the job. Keeping the paddle in a case does the rest, and matters far more.
  • A robot. Excellent equipment and the wrong first purchase — see whether a table tennis robot is worth it. You need a training habit before you need a training machine.
  • Multiple paddles for yourself. One paddle you know well beats three you swap between.

What This Actually Costs

Realistic totals, assuming you own a dining table and nothing else:

  • Minimum viable — an all-in-one set with net, paddles and balls: around $40.
  • Sensible household setup — a good retractable net, a four-paddle set and a box of 3-star balls: around $85.
  • One serious player plus guests — a good net, one $50 paddle, a cheap four-set and bulk balls: around $115.

For comparison, a full-size table starts around $300 and needs a room you probably do not have. The $85 version of this sport is not a stripped-down version of it — for most people it is the whole thing.

The Short Version

Buy a net before you buy anything else, and skip the table entirely unless you have a garage. Get one good paddle if you are the only real player, or a four-paddle set if you are equipping a household. Buy more balls than seems reasonable. Then play for two months before spending another penny — by then you will know exactly what is worth upgrading, and it will probably not be what you expected.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a ping pong table to play at home?

No. A retractable net clamps onto a dining table, kitchen island or desk and turns it into a court in under a minute. The playing surface is smaller than regulation, so rallies are faster and more about hand speed than footwork, but it is real ping pong. This is how a large share of casual players actually play, and it costs about $25 rather than $300 plus a spare room.

How much does it cost to start playing ping pong?

About $40 for an all-in-one set with a net, paddles and balls if you already have a table to clamp to. Around $85 buys a noticeably better setup: a quality retractable net, a four-paddle set and a box of 3-star balls. A full-size table pushes it past $300 and requires roughly 19 feet of room length to play on properly.

Should I buy one good paddle or a set of four?

Buy one good paddle if you are the main player and everyone else is an occasional guest, because one $50 paddle improves your own game more than four $12 ones. Buy a set of four if you are equipping a household or common room, since four set paddles usually cost less than a single mid-range paddle and nobody has to wait for a bat.

How many ping pong balls do I need?

A box of six 3-star balls is enough for casual games. If you do any drills or own a robot, buy a hundred at a time — a robot hopper empties in a few minutes and stopping to collect balls destroys the point of the session. Balls crack from being stepped on rather than from play, so assume steady attrition either way.

What ping pong equipment can I skip as a beginner?

Table tennis shoes, rubber cleaner, protective film, spare paddles for yourself, and a training robot. All are real products with real uses at club level, and none of them will improve a beginner's game as much as simply playing more. The only accessory worth early money is a ball collector, and only once you have started practising rather than just playing.
