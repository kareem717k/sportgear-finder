Blades & Rubbers vs Pre-Made Paddles

Updated August 2026 ֲ· 8 min read ֲ· Affiliate links may earn us a commission

Home / Ping Pong / Blades & Rubbers vs Pre-Made Paddles

Every serious table tennis player eventually stops buying paddles and starts buying parts. It looks like an enthusiast affectation from the outside and it is not — it is cheaper over time, it lets you tune each side of the bat independently, and it is the only way to keep a paddle at its best as rubber wears.

The question is when. Switch too early and you are guessing with extra steps. Switch too late and you spend years paying for whole paddles when you needed one $25 sheet of rubber.

What a Custom Setup Actually Is

A blade — the wooden handle and face, often with carbon layers inside — plus two sheets of rubber, one per side, which you glue on yourself. That is it. A pre-made paddle is the same three components, assembled by a factory to a price.

The difference is that you choose each part. A grippy, spin-heavy rubber on the forehand for looping and a faster, more forgiving one on the backhand for blocking is a completely normal setup, and no pre-made paddle offers it.

The Switching Point

There is a reasonably clear test: can you consistently loop against backspin, and do you have an opinion about what your current paddle does wrong?

If the answer to either is no, stay with pre-made paddles. Before that point the equipment is not what is limiting you, and the money goes further on balls, a net and playing time. You also have no basis for choosing between rubbers, so you will pick by price or by what a forum said.

If the answer to both is yes, switch. From there the ability to replace one worn sheet instead of a whole paddle saves real money every year.

What It Costs

A sensible first custom setup runs roughly $110 to $180 all in — a blade in the $50 to $110 range plus two rubbers at $20 to $40 each. Elite setups pass $300, mostly because top rubbers are $70 to $90 per sheet.

That looks worse than a $50 pre-made paddle until you account for wear. Rubber loses grip in six to twelve months of regular play. On a pre-made paddle, re-rubbering costs about what the paddle did, so most people buy a new paddle instead and throw away a perfectly good blade. On a custom setup you replace one sheet for $25 and the blade lasts a decade. Over three years the custom route is usually cheaper.

Choosing a Blade

Buy the blade for the game you want in two years, because it is the part you keep. The main decision is wood versus carbon, and where the carbon sits.

  • All-wood flexes on contact. More dwell time, more feel, more control, less raw speed. The safe choice and still what many top players use.
  • Outer-ply carbon puts the carbon just under the rubber. Very fast, very stiff, huge sweet spot, minimal feedback on soft shots. Flatters power, punishes touch.
  • Inner-ply carbon puts it closer to the core, so the outer wood flexes first. Wood-like feel on slow shots, carbon power on hard ones. The most popular choice for all-round attacking play, and the one most players settle on.

Choosing Rubbers: Tacky vs Tensor

This is the decision that changes your game most, and it splits along a genuine geographic divide.

Tacky Chinese rubbers, such as the Hurricane family, have a top sheet that physically sticks to the ball — hold one upside down with a ball on it and the ball stays. That produces enormous spin on slow, brushing loops. The sponge underneath is hard and gives very little back, so all the speed has to come from your arm. Superb for a player with fast, committed strokes; miserable for anyone else.

European and Japanese tensor rubbers are pre-stretched so the sponge stores and returns energy. Speed comes more easily, the feel is more forgiving, and spin is generated by the sponge as much as the surface. Easier to use, less spin ceiling on slow brushing contact.

The common compromise — tacky forehand, tensor backhand — exists because the forehand is where you loop with a big swing and the backhand is where you need speed without one.

One more spec: sponge thickness, usually 1.5mm to 2.1mm, with sponge plus top sheet capped at 4mm under the rules. Thicker means more speed and spin and less control. If you do not know, 2.0mm is the standard answer.

Gluing Is Easier Than You Think

Thin coat of water-based table tennis glue on both the blade face and the rubber. Let both dry until clear — about fifteen minutes. Lay the rubber down from one edge, press out from the middle to avoid bubbles, then trim around the blade with sharp scissors. Twenty minutes the first time, ten after that.

Two warnings. Use water-based glue only: speed glues and boosters are banned in competition, give off solvent fumes and warp blades. And do not use household adhesives — they will not come off, and removing a rubber cleanly is how you get to re-use a blade for a decade.

The Short Version

Stay with pre-made paddles until you can loop against backspin and have a specific complaint about your current bat. At that point buy an inner-ply carbon or all-wood blade you will not outgrow, put a tacky rubber on the forehand and a tensor on the backhand, and expect to spend $110 to $180. After that you replace $25 sheets rather than $60 paddles, and the blade outlasts the decade.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I switch from a pre-made paddle to a custom setup?

When you can consistently loop against backspin and you have a specific opinion about what your current paddle does wrong. Before that the equipment is not your limiting factor and you have no basis for choosing between rubbers. After it, being able to select and replace each side independently is a real and continuing advantage.

How much does it cost to build your own table tennis paddle?

Roughly $110 to $180 for a sensible first setup: a blade between $50 and $110, plus two rubbers at $20 to $40 each. Elite setups exceed $300, largely because top rubbers cost $70 to $90 per sheet. Ongoing cost is lower than a pre-made paddle, since you replace individual rubber sheets rather than the whole bat.

What is the difference between inner and outer carbon blades?

It is where the carbon layer sits. Outer-ply carbon sits just beneath the rubber, making the blade fast and stiff on contact with maximum speed and minimum feel. Inner-ply carbon sits nearer the core so the outer wood flexes first, giving a softer, wood-like touch on slow shots and carbon power on hard ones. Inner-ply is the more popular all-round choice.

What is the difference between tacky Chinese and European tensor rubbers?

Tacky Chinese rubbers have a top sheet that physically sticks to the ball, producing huge spin on slow brushing loops, but a hard sponge that gives nothing back, so you supply all the speed. European and Japanese tensor rubbers are pre-stretched so the sponge returns energy, making speed easier and the feel more forgiving. Many players use tacky on the forehand and tensor on the backhand.

How do I glue rubber to a table tennis blade?

Use water-based table tennis glue, applied in a thin coat to both the blade face and the rubber. Let both dry until clear, roughly fifteen minutes, then lay the rubber down from one edge and press outward from the middle to avoid bubbles. Trim around the blade with sharp scissors. Never use speed glues or boosters, which are banned in competition and damage blades.
