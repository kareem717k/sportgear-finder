Ski Gear: Complete Beginner Guide

Updated September 2026 ֲ· 9 min read ֲ· Affiliate links may earn us a commission

Home / Ski / Ski Gear: Complete Beginner Guide

Skiing has the worst beginner-gear problem of any sport we cover. The equipment is expensive, the shops are enthusiastic, and the thing you are told to buy first - skis - is the thing you should buy last. Meanwhile the items that genuinely change how your first week feels cost less than a lift pass between them.

This is the order that actually makes sense: rent the hardware, own the things that touch you. Skis, boots and bindings stay rented until you know what kind of skier you are. Goggles, a helmet, gloves and socks you buy immediately, because they are cheap, they are personal, and the rental versions of them are genuinely unpleasant.

Rent the Skis. Rent Them for Longer Than You Think.

A beginner cannot tell a good ski from a bad one, which is not an insult - the differences between a carving ski, an all-mountain ski and a park ski only become legible once you can reliably link turns. Buying before then means buying on colour, price or a shop assistant's commission, and skis are the single most expensive thing on the mountain.

Renting also means you ski a different ski each trip, which is how you find out what you actually like. And modern rental fleets are genuinely good - resorts replace them every year or two, so a rental ski is frequently newer than one a keen amateur owns. Most people should be three or four weeks into skiing before buying skis, and many never need to.

Rent the Boots Too - Until You Can Afford a Proper Fitting

Ski boots are the exception to every rule about gear, because they are the one item where fit is everything and where buying online is genuinely a bad idea. A boot is sized in Mondopoint - the length of your foot in centimetres - but two boots in the same Mondo size can differ enormously in width, volume and how the cuff sits against your calf.

The right way to buy boots is an hour in a shop with a bootfitter who measures your foot, watches you flex, and heat-moulds the liner. That costs money and cannot be replicated by a size chart. Until you are skiing enough weeks a year to justify it, rent - and if rental boots hurt, go back and ask them to change the size rather than enduring it, which is what almost every beginner does.

Buy These Four, Straight Away

Everything in this list is cheap relative to a ski holiday, personal enough that renting is unpleasant, and makes a difference you will notice on day one.

  • Goggles - rental goggles are scratched, and a scratched lens in flat light is genuinely dangerous. This is the item that most changes your day.
  • A helmet - rental helmets have unknown impact histories, and a helmet that has taken a hit is finished. Yours costs about the price of three days' rental.
  • Gloves - nobody rents gloves. Cold wet hands end more beginner days than cold feet.
  • Ski socks - the cheapest item here and the one that fixes the most complaints. One thin merino pair, never two.

The Three Mistakes Almost Every Beginner Makes

Wearing two pairs of socks

The reasoning is intuitive and completely wrong. A ski boot is fitted with a specific amount of space around your foot, and that space is what lets blood circulate and keep your toes warm. Two socks fill it, the boot squeezes, circulation drops and your feet get colder. The friction between the two layers is also where blisters start. Wear one thin merino sock.

Wearing a cotton t-shirt under the jacket

You work hard going down, sweat into the cotton, then sit still on a chairlift in the wind wearing a wet layer against your skin. The evaporation pulls heat out of you at exactly the moment you have stopped producing any. Any cheap synthetic base layer fixes it for very little money - see merino versus synthetic for which kind to buy.

Buying the darkest goggle lens

Dark lenses look good and work beautifully in spring sunshine. In flat light - cloud, fog, falling snow, which is most of a European winter - they flatten the terrain into a featureless white sheet and hide every bump. If you can only own one lens, buy one in the 20 to 40 percent VLT range. The lens tint guide has the numbers.

The short version

Rent skis and boots until you have three or four weeks under you and can afford a proper bootfitting. Buy goggles, a helmet, gloves and socks before your first day - they cost a fraction of the holiday and they are the four things that decide whether you are comfortable. Do not wear two pairs of socks, do not wear cotton, and do not buy the darkest lens.

What to Add Next

Once the basics are covered and you are skiing more than one trip a year, the next purchases are a merino base layer set, a second goggle lens for low light, and a boot bag if you are flying. Boots come after that, from a fitter. Skis come last, if at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should a beginner buy or rent skis?

Rent, for at least the first three or four weeks of skiing. A beginner cannot yet tell a carving ski from an all-mountain ski, so buying means choosing on price or colour, and skis are the most expensive item on the mountain. Renting also lets you try something different each trip, which is how you find out what suits you. Resort rental fleets are replaced every year or two, so the ski is often newer than one a keen amateur owns.

What ski gear should I buy first?

Goggles, a helmet, gloves and ski socks. All four are cheap relative to the cost of a ski holiday, all four are personal enough that renting them is unpleasant or impossible, and all four change how your first day feels. Rental goggles are scratched, rental helmets have unknown impact histories, and nobody rents gloves or socks at all.

How much should a first ski kit cost?

Goggles, a helmet, gloves and socks together come to roughly $120 to $160 at the budget end of our picks, and that covers everything you should own as a beginner. Add a synthetic base layer set for about $17 more. Skis, boots and bindings stay rented, which is where the real money would otherwise go.

Do I need to buy ski boots as a beginner?

No, and buying them online is a genuinely bad idea. Boots are the one item where fit decides everything, and two boots in the same Mondopoint size can differ substantially in width and volume. Buying properly means an hour with a bootfitter who measures your foot and heat-moulds the liner. Rent until you ski enough to justify that, and if rental boots hurt, go back and change the size rather than enduring them.

What is the most common beginner ski gear mistake?

Wearing two pairs of socks. The logic seems sound and it is exactly backwards: a boot is fitted with a set amount of space for circulation, and a second sock fills it, so the boot compresses your foot and your toes get colder. The friction between the layers also causes blisters. One thin merino sock is the correct answer, every time.
