The ball decides how the game feels more than any other piece of kit. Three things separate a $28 ball from an $85 one: cover material (rubber for the backyard, composite microfibre for club play, premium composite for match balls), panel construction (18-panel and dimpled surfaces fly straighter than cheap 18-panel glue-ups), and approval level (NFHS for US high school, USAV/NCAA for club and college, FIVB for international). Buy for the surface you actually play on — a beach ball indoors feels dead, and an indoor ball on sand is ruined in a summer.

How to Choose a Volleyball

Match the Ball to the Surface

Indoor balls are firmer, heavier and built for a fast, predictable flight on a wooden court. Beach and outdoor balls are a touch larger, lighter and softer with a weather-resistant cover so wind and bare forearms are less of a problem. Using either on the wrong surface costs you both performance and the cover.

Check the Approval You Actually Need

NFHS covers US high school play, USAV and NCAA cover club and college, and FIVB is the international standard. Approval mostly buys tighter manufacturing tolerances — roundness, weight and rebound consistency. If nobody is checking, an NFHS composite ball plays close enough to a match ball that the extra $40 is better spent on shoes or knee pads.

Composite vs Leather vs Rubber

Rubber balls are for the backyard and nothing else. Composite microfibre is the workhorse: soft, durable and cheap enough to buy several. Premium leather covers like the Molten Super Touch give the best touch for setters, but they need a clean indoor court to survive.

Buy a Pump at the Same Time

Almost every ball on this page ships deflated. Inflate to the pressure printed next to the valve — usually 4.3 to 4.6 psi indoors — and check it monthly. Over-inflation is the fastest way to turn a good ball into a hard one.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between an indoor and an outdoor volleyball?

Indoor balls are heavier, harder and made from composite or genuine leather so they travel fast and hold a predictable flight on a hard court. Outdoor and beach balls are slightly larger, softer and lighter with a water-resistant cover, so wind affects them less violently and they hurt less on bare forearms. Playing indoors with a beach ball feels sluggish; playing on sand with an indoor ball ruins the cover.

Do I need an FIVB-approved ball?

Only if you are playing sanctioned matches that require one. FIVB approval certifies the ball meets international size, weight, roundness and rebound tolerances, and those balls cost $65 or more. For club practice, school PE and recreational play, an NFHS-approved composite ball at $35 to $50 plays close to the same and lasts longer through abuse.

What pressure should a volleyball be inflated to?

Most indoor volleyballs are rated at 4.3 to 4.6 psi (0.30 to 0.325 kg/cm²). Almost every ball ships deflated, so you need a pump and a gauge. Over-inflating is the single most common way to wreck a ball: it stretches the panels, hardens the touch and shortens the life of the bladder.

How long does a volleyball last?

A composite indoor ball used three or four times a week typically holds its touch for one to two seasons. Sand, grit and UV are what kill balls fastest, so keep outdoor balls outdoors and indoor balls indoors, wipe the cover down after play, and store them partly deflated in the off season.
