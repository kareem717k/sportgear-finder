The training aids worth owning do one of two jobs: they give you repetitions you cannot get without a partner (tethered rebounders return the ball after every serve or swing), or they block one specific fault (a passing trainer that stops you bending your arms, a net extender that punishes a low contact point). Everything on this page is from Tandem Sport, which has made these tools since 1989 and is what most US club programmes actually own. Buy for a named problem — an aid you bought "to get better" ends up in the garage.

How to Choose Volleyball Training Aids

Buy for a Named Fault

The aids that get used solve a problem the player can describe: serves that do not clear the net, arms that bend on the pass, spikes hit into the tape. If you cannot name the fault, spend the money on court time instead.

Repetition Beats Everything

The tethered tools — the Volleyball Pal and Spike Pal — are the highest-value items here because they remove ball chasing. A player alone in a driveway can hit 150 serves in half an hour instead of 40. That volume, not the gadget, is what improves the skill.

Individual vs Team Purchases

Under $50 is an individual purchase. The spike trainers are station equipment: they make sense for a coach, a club or a household with several players, and they are wasted sitting in one player's garage.

Check What You Can Anchor To

The cord-based trainers need something solid overhead — a basketball hoop, a pole, a beam. Confirm you have one before ordering, because the alternative is a $40 elastic cord with nowhere to hang it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do volleyball training aids actually help?

The useful ones solve a specific problem: they give you repetitions you cannot get alone (serve and spike rebounders), or they stop one particular technical fault (arm-bending on a pass, hitting into the net). They do not teach a skill from scratch and they do not replace playing. Buy an aid for a fault you can name, not as general improvement.

What is the best training aid for practising serves alone?

A tethered rebounder like the Volleyball Pal — a waist strap, an elastic cord and a ball pouch — returns the ball after every contact so you can hit 100 serves in the time it takes to chase down 20. It is the single highest-repetition tool for a player practising alone in a garden or empty gym.

How do I stop hitting the ball into the net?

Usually it is contact point, not power: the hand meets the ball in front of and below the ideal window. A spike trainer that holds the ball at a fixed height lets you groove a full arm swing with contact at the top of the reach, and a net extender forces you to hit over a higher barrier so the real net feels low.

Are training aids worth it for a beginner?

One is. A passing trainer or a serve rebounder gives a new player thousands of clean repetitions of the two skills that decide whether they enjoy the sport. The bigger hitting rigs are better bought by a club or a family with several players, because they are built for a station in a practice rotation.
