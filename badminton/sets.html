A badminton set is the right purchase in exactly one situation: when you need more than one racket at once and nobody involved has a strong opinion about balance points yet. Buying two matched rackets in a box costs less than buying them separately, the covers and shuttles are thrown in, and everyone gets the same equipment so nobody blames the racket. The catch is that sets are where manufacturers hide their worst frames, so the useful question is not "which set is cheapest" but "which set contains rackets I would have bought on their own". These six do.

How to Choose a Badminton Set

Count the Rackets Before You Compare the Prices

The single biggest source of confusion in this category is that "badminton set" means two entirely different products: a pair of rackets in a bag, and a four-racket-plus-net garden kit. A $40 two-racket set and a $50 four-racket-plus-net set are not competing with each other, and the second one is not better value — it is spending the same money across twice as many frames plus a net, which is exactly where the quality goes. Decide how many people are playing first.

Aluminium and Steel Sets Are Fine, Within Limits

Most sets under $45 use an aluminium head on a steel shaft. That construction is heavier than graphite and flexes in the wrong place, so shots feel mushy and the racket arrives late — but it is also nearly indestructible, which is genuinely the right property for equipment that lives in a garage and gets lent to guests. Buy aluminium if the set is for occasional social play. Buy graphite if anyone using it intends to improve.

Carbon Two-Player Sets Are the Hidden Value Here

The interesting products in this category are the pairs of full-carbon rackets sold at roughly the price of one mid-range racket plus twenty dollars. If two people in a household both play properly, this is the cheapest way to equip both of them with frames that will not need replacing in a year. Check the weight class is stated — a set that will not tell you whether it is 4U or 5U is usually hiding an unpleasant answer.

What Is Actually in the Box

Sets vary wildly in what comes with them, and the shuttles included are almost always the cheapest hard nylon available. Treat any bundled shuttles as a temporary loan and budget for a proper tube separately — our shuttlecocks page covers the difference. The carry bag included with most sets is worth having; the grips are not, and one cheap overgrip transforms how a set racket feels in the hand.

When Not to Buy a Set

If you are buying for one person who has already played a few times and knows they enjoy it, skip this page entirely. A single $35 to $40 racket chosen for balance and flex will be better than either racket in a $45 pair, and you will not be carrying a spare you never use. Sets solve the problem of equipping a group, not the problem of equipping a player.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a badminton set better value than buying rackets separately?

For two or more players, usually yes — a matched pair typically costs 20 to 30 percent less than the same two rackets bought individually, and the covers and bag are included. For one player it is worse value, because half the money goes on a racket that sits in a cupboard. The exception is if you want a spare in your bag for a broken string mid-session, which is a real reason club players own pairs.

Do badminton sets come with a net?

Some do and some do not, and the listings are not always clear about it. Two-player and four-player racket sets normally include rackets, shuttles and a bag but no net. Full garden kits include a net and posts but usually spend so little on the rackets that they are barely playable. If you want both, buying a good racket set and a separate net from our <a href="nets.html">nets and posts</a> page gives a much better result for similar money.

Are the shuttlecocks included with a set any good?

Almost never. Bundled shuttles are the cheapest hard nylon on the market, chosen to make the box look full rather than to fly properly. They will work for a first afternoon. Budget ten to fifteen dollars for a tube of decent nylon shuttles alongside any set — it makes more difference to how the game feels than anything else you could spend the same money on.

Can adults use a four-player family badminton set?

Yes, but the rackets are usually standard adult length rather than junior, so children under about eight will find them long and heavy rather than the other way round. If you are buying for a mixed-age group, look for a set that states a lighter weight class, and accept that the same rackets will feel slightly underpowered to any adult who has played properly before.

What should I upgrade first on a cheap badminton set?

The shuttles, then the grip, then the rackets — in that order. Proper nylon shuttles cost about ten dollars and change how the game plays immediately. A two-dollar overgrip fixes the slippery plastic handle every cheap set ships with. Only after both of those is a racket upgrade the thing holding you back, and by then you will know what balance you want.
