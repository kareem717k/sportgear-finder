Cold hands end ski days earlier than cold anything else, and the cause is usually not the insulation rating. It is wet, and it is fit. A glove that soaks through at the cuff stops insulating within an hour no matter how it was marketed, and a glove tight enough to squeeze the fingertips cuts off the circulation that was supposed to warm them. Those two failures account for most miserable hands on a mountain. The choice between gloves and mittens is the other real decision: mittens are meaningfully warmer because your fingers share heat, gloves let you work a zip or a buckle without taking them off. Below are six of each kind that hold up.

How to Choose Ski Gloves

Mittens Are Warmer. Gloves Are More Useful. Pick Your Problem.

In a mitten your fingers sit in one chamber and warm each other, which is worth more than any amount of extra insulation in a five-fingered glove. If your hands run genuinely cold, a mid-price mitten will beat an expensive glove every time. The cost is dexterity: you cannot easily work a boot buckle, a zip, a phone or a lift pass without taking it off, and taking it off is how gloves get dropped from chairlifts. Many people end up owning a mitten for deep cold and a glove for spring.

"Waterproof" Covers Two Very Different Things

A cheap glove is waterproof because the outer fabric has a coating on it, and that coating wears off at the palm and the fingertips — the places you touch snow. A membrane glove has a waterproof-breathable layer like Gore-Tex laminated inside, which does not rub away and also lets sweat vapour out so your hand is not damp from the inside. The difference shows up on the fourth wet day, not the first. If you ski in maritime snow — the Alps in spring, the Pacific Northwest, Scotland — the membrane is the upgrade to make.

The Cuff Decides More Than the Insulation

A gauntlet cuff is the long one that goes over your jacket sleeve and cinches down. It is what keeps snow out when you fall, and falling is how snow gets in. An undercuff is the short one that tucks inside the sleeve; it is less bulky and fine on a groomed resort day, provided your jacket cuff seals well. If you are learning, falling often, or skiing powder, buy the gauntlet — a single faceplant packs snow into a short cuff and the day is effectively over.

Too Tight Is Colder Than Too Thin

Insulation works by trapping still air, and a glove that is snug on your fingers has no air to trap. Worse, pressure on the fingers restricts the blood flow doing the actual warming. If you are between sizes, take the larger one. The same logic explains a trick that surprises people: adding a thin liner glove inside a slightly loose glove is warmer than wearing a thicker glove on its own, and it gives you something to keep on when you take the outer off.

Leashes Are Not Optional Above the Treeline

A glove dropped from a chairlift is gone. Wrist leashes look fussy in the shop and stop being funny the first time you watch a mitten fall two hundred feet into a gully. Every value and premium pick here has one or accepts one. Use it on the lift, particularly if you are taking a glove off to handle a phone.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are mittens really warmer than ski gloves?

Yes, and it is not a small difference. Fingers in one chamber share heat and have far less surface area losing it, so a mid-priced mitten typically outperforms a more expensive glove of similar construction. The trade is dexterity: buckles, zips, phones and lift passes all become two-handed operations or require taking the mitten off. If your hands run cold, buy the mitten.

What does Gore-Tex actually do in a ski glove?

It is a waterproof, breathable membrane laminated inside the glove rather than a coating sprayed on the outside. That means it does not wear off at the palm and fingertips where you contact snow, and it lets sweat vapour escape so your hand does not end up damp from the inside. The practical difference appears on long wet days and over multiple seasons, not on a single cold dry morning.

Should I buy gauntlet or undercuff ski gloves?

Gauntlet if you fall often, ski powder, or are still learning - the long cuff cinches over your jacket sleeve and keeps snow out when you go down. Undercuff if you ski groomed runs in reliable conditions and prefer less bulk under a well-sealed jacket cuff. A single fall with a short cuff packs snow against your wrist and effectively ends the day.

Why are my hands still cold in expensive ski gloves?

Usually fit or damp, not insulation. A glove that squeezes your fingertips has no trapped air to insulate with and restricts the circulation that warms them, so tighter is colder. If the glove is wet - from snow through the cuff or sweat trapped inside a non-breathable shell - it stops insulating almost entirely. Try a size up, add a thin liner, and check that the cuff actually seals.

Do I need ski-specific gloves or will winter gloves do?

For a first day or two, warm waterproof winter gloves are survivable. The differences that matter are the cuff design, a reinforced palm that resists wet abrasion from snow and edges, and a wrist leash. Ordinary winter gloves have short cuffs, soak at the palm quickly and have nothing to stop them falling off a chairlift, which is why the cheap end of this page still beats them.
