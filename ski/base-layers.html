A base layer has one job: move sweat off your skin so that when you stop moving, you are not wearing it. Skiing is unusually hard on this, because it alternates between hard work going down and sitting still in the wind going up, several times an hour. Get it right and you barely notice the cold; get it wrong and you spend the lift chilled in a damp shirt. There is really only one rule — no cotton — and then one choice, merino or synthetic. Merino handles smell and stays warm when damp; synthetic dries faster and costs a fraction as much. Below are six of both, and a straight answer on when the wool is worth it.

How to Choose a Ski Base Layer

The Only Real Mistake Is Cotton

Cotton absorbs sweat and holds it. On a mountain that means you work hard down a run, soak a cotton shirt, then sit on a chairlift in the wind wearing a wet layer against your skin, and the evaporation pulls heat out of you exactly when you have stopped generating it. There is an old hill-walking phrase — cotton kills — and while a resort day is not that serious, the mechanism is real and it is why people who dress warmly still end up cold. Everything on this page is synthetic or wool. That alone is most of the upgrade.

Merino Versus Synthetic, Honestly

Merino keeps insulating when damp, resists odour so well that three days is genuinely fine, and feels better against skin than most synthetics. Synthetic wicks faster, dries much faster, costs a quarter as much and survives being tumble dried. For a week-long trip where washing is awkward, merino earns its price — you can wear it repeatedly without offending anyone. For a few days a season near home, a good synthetic set does the same thermal job for a fraction of the money. Neither is wrong; they solve different problems.

Fit Snug, Not Tight

A base layer moves moisture by contact, so it has to touch your skin over most of its area — a loose one leaves gaps where sweat sits instead of wicking. It should feel like a second skin rather than compression gear. Too tight restricts movement and, at the wrist and ankle, circulation. The other thing to check is length: a top that rides up when you reach for a lift bar leaves a cold strip at your back all day, so look for a longer cut or a tail.

Fleece-Lined Is Warmer and Wicks Less Well

Most budget "thermal" sets are a synthetic face with a brushed fleece inside. The fleece traps air and is genuinely warm, but it is also slower to move moisture than a smooth technical knit, so it suits cold static days better than hard skiing. If you run hot or ski aggressively, a thinner non-fleeced layer with a proper mid-layer over it will work better than one thick fleecy one. Layering beats bulk, in base layers as everywhere else.

Bottoms Matter Less Than You Think, Until They Do

Legs generate heat and modern ski trousers are well insulated, so plenty of people ski comfortably with nothing under them. Where base layer bottoms earn their place is cold lift-heavy days, spring slush where your trousers wet out, and anyone who feels the cold in the thighs on a long exposed chair. Buying the set is usually cheaper than buying the top alone, which is why most of this page is two-piece — but if you only wear one piece, make it the top.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I wear under ski trousers?

A thin non-cotton base layer, or in many cases nothing at all. Legs produce a lot of heat and ski trousers are already insulated, so base layer bottoms matter most on cold, lift-heavy days or if you feel the cold in your thighs. What you should not wear is cotton leggings or jeans - they hold sweat and slush water against the skin and stay cold all day.

Is merino or synthetic better for ski base layers?

Merino if you are travelling and cannot wash easily - it resists odour for several days and keeps insulating when damp. Synthetic if you ski close to home and can launder between days - it wicks and dries faster and costs roughly a quarter as much. Thermally they are close enough that the choice comes down to trip length, budget and how your skin feels about wool.

How many layers should I wear skiing?

Three is the standard: a thin base layer against the skin to move sweat, a mid layer such as fleece or a light down piece for insulation, and a waterproof shell against wind and snow. The point of three thin layers rather than one thick coat is that you can remove the middle one when the sun comes out. Adding a fourth layer usually just restricts movement.

Can I wear a cotton t-shirt under my ski jacket?

You can, but it is the single most common reason people are cold while dressed warmly. Cotton soaks up sweat on the way down and holds it against your skin on the lift back up, where wind evaporates it and takes your body heat with it. Any cheap synthetic base layer - including the budget picks on this page - fixes it for very little money.

How tight should a ski base layer be?

Snug enough to be in contact with your skin almost everywhere, because that contact is how it moves moisture, but not compressive. If it bags anywhere, sweat pools there instead of wicking. Check the length as well: a top that pulls out of your waistband when you reach up for a chairlift bar leaves a cold gap at the small of your back for the rest of the day.
