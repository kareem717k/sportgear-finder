Running shoes are the most common and most expensive mistake in pickleball. They are built to move you forwards and have soft, tall midsoles that roll over sideways — exactly the load pickleball puts on you every single point. A court shoe sits lower, has a wider and flatter base, a reinforced upper that resists lateral shear, and a non-marking outsole. If you play more than once a week, this is the piece of kit most likely to keep you off the physio's table.

How to Choose Pickleball Shoes

Never Play in Running Shoes

Running shoes have narrow, tall, soft midsoles designed for forward motion. Pickleball is almost entirely lateral: split steps, side shuffles, sudden stops. A running shoe rolls at the ankle under that load, and the outsole rubber is not built for the abrasion of a hard court. Rolled ankles and shredded soles are both avoidable with a proper court shoe.

Indoor and Outdoor Wear Differently

Outdoor courts are abrasive concrete or asphalt and destroy soft outsoles fast. Indoor gym floors are far gentler but demand a genuinely non-marking sole that grips a polished surface. If you play mostly outdoors, prioritise outsole durability — some manufacturers offer a six-month outsole warranty, which tells you where they expect the wear.

Fit Wide If You Are Between Sizes

Court shoes are cut narrower than running shoes because a snug fit stops your foot sliding inside the shoe during lateral moves. That is correct, but it means anyone with a wider foot should look specifically for a 2E or wide fitting rather than sizing up, which just makes the shoe longer without adding width where you need it.

Replace Them Before They Look Finished

The support in a court shoe fails before the upper looks worn. If the midsole has compressed and the shoe feels flat, or you can twist it easily along its length, the lateral support has gone even if the outsole still has tread. Playing three times a week, expect six to nine months outdoors.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use tennis shoes for pickleball?

Yes, and it is the most sensible substitute. Tennis and pickleball make almost identical demands: lateral movement, sudden stops, hard court abrasion. Most dedicated pickleball shoes are lightly modified tennis shoes. What you should not use is a running, walking or gym trainer.

What is the difference between indoor and outdoor pickleball shoes?

Mostly outsole rubber. Outdoor shoes use a harder, more abrasion-resistant compound and a more aggressive tread to survive concrete. Indoor shoes use a softer gum-style non-marking rubber that grips polished wood. An outdoor shoe works indoors but may feel slippery; an indoor shoe outdoors will wear through quickly.

How long do pickleball shoes last?

Playing three or four times a week outdoors, six to nine months is realistic. Indoors they last considerably longer. Judge by support rather than looks: if the shoe twists easily or the midsole feels flat and dead, it has stopped protecting you regardless of how much tread is left.

Do I need wide pickleball shoes?

If you normally buy wide shoes, yes — court shoes are cut deliberately narrow to keep your foot locked in place, so a standard fitting will be tighter than you expect. Sizing up in a standard width is the wrong fix, because it adds length without adding the width you actually need.

Are expensive pickleball shoes worth it?

For durability, usually yes. The gap between a budget and a premium court shoe shows up in how long the outsole survives outdoor concrete and how long the midsole keeps its support. If you play once a week indoors, a budget court shoe is fine. Four times a week on concrete and the premium option often works out cheaper per month.
