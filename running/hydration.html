How to Choose Running Hydration

Do You Need to Carry Water at All?

For runs under about an hour in mild conditions, most healthy runners do not need to drink during the run — arriving hydrated is enough. Carry water on runs longer than that, in heat and humidity, and on trails without a water source. Thirst is a reasonable guide for most people. If a route passes fountains or shops, planning around them is lighter than carrying anything.

Handheld, Belt or Vest

A handheld is a bottle with a strap that holds it to your hand; it is simple, cheap and easy to drink from, and good for up to 90 minutes or so. A hydration belt carries small bottles on your waist, so your hands are free, but belts with bottles can bounce. A vest carries soft flasks on the chest or a bladder on the back, holds the most and moves the least, and has space for a jacket and food — the standard for marathon training and trail running.

Soft Flasks Beat Hard Bottles

A soft flask collapses as you drink, so it does not slosh and it does not get in the way when half-empty. That is why the premium picks here use them. They are quieter and more comfortable, and they roll up small when empty. Hard bottles are cheaper, easier to clean and more durable. Insulated bottles like the Nathan SpeedDraw keep water cool on hot runs.

Bladder vs Flasks in a Vest

A bladder on the back carries a lot — one and a half to two litres — and suits long unsupported trail days. Front flasks are easier to refill at aid stations, let you see how much you have left, and keep the weight on the front where it balances better. Most race vests are built around front flasks, with a bladder sleeve as an option.

Fit Decides Whether You Will Use It

A vest should fit like a snug waistcoat, with the chest straps holding it still without restricting breathing. Try it with the flasks full and run a few strides before deciding it works; a vest that is comfortable empty can bounce when loaded. Wash bladders and flasks after every use, and dry them fully, or they will grow mould faster than you would believe.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I carry water on a run?

On runs longer than about an hour, in hot or humid weather, and on trails without water along the route. For shorter runs in mild conditions most people do not need to drink during the run, provided they start well hydrated. Thirst is a reasonable guide for most healthy runners.

Is a hydration vest or belt better?

A vest carries more, bounces less and has room for food and a jacket, which is why it is the standard for marathon training and trail running. A belt is lighter and cooler for shorter runs where you need only a little water. If you run long regularly, a vest is the better investment.

How much water should I carry on a long run?

A common starting point is roughly 400 to 800 ml per hour, adjusted for heat, body size and sweat rate - but individual needs vary a lot. Plan refills around fountains or shops where you can, carry more on hot days and remote trails, and consider electrolytes on runs over about two hours.

Are handheld water bottles annoying to run with?

Less than people expect. A good handheld has a strap that holds the bottle to your palm, so you do not have to grip it, and most runners stop noticing it after a few runs. Swap hands occasionally to avoid asymmetry. For runs over about 90 minutes, a vest or belt becomes more practical.

How do I clean a hydration bladder?

Rinse it after every use, then wash it with warm soapy water or a cleaning tablet and let it dry completely with the bladder propped open. Mould grows quickly in a bladder left damp. Storing a clean, dry bladder in the freezer is an old trick that also stops growth between uses.
