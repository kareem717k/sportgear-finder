Shoes are the one piece of running kit that actually matters, and the one where the marketing is loudest. Strip it back and there are only two questions. First, neutral or stability — does your foot roll inward enough on landing that you want a shoe that resists it? Most runners do not, and a neutral shoe is the right default. Second, how much cushion — which depends far more on how many miles you run a week than on how serious you are. Beyond that, the right shoe is the one that fits your foot, and no review can tell you that. Below are six that are sensible at their price, from a first pair to a high-mileage cruiser. The links go to the men's listing; every model here is also made in a women's version, and three come in wide fittings. New to it? Start with the beginner gear guide.

How to Choose Running Shoes

Neutral Is the Right Default

Every foot rolls inward a little when it lands — that is pronation, and it is how the foot absorbs shock. A stability shoe adds firmer material or guide rails on the inside to limit that roll. The research on prescribing shoes by foot type is weaker than the shoe wall suggests, and most runners do best in a neutral shoe chosen for comfort. If you have a history of shin, knee or arch pain that neutral shoes have not solved, or a shop has watched you run and your ankles visibly collapse, try a stability shoe like the Adrenaline. Otherwise, do not go looking for a problem to fix.

Cushion Follows Mileage, Not Ambition

A beginner running three 20-minute sessions a week does not need a maximum-cushion shoe, and will often feel less connected to the ground in one. As weekly distance climbs past about 20 miles, more foam genuinely helps, because the same legs are absorbing far more impacts. That is the whole logic of this page: the budget shoes are moderate, the Ghost is the middle, and the Bondi is for people stacking up miles or protecting sore joints.

Fit Beats Every Other Specification

Leave roughly a thumb's width between your longest toe and the end of the shoe, because feet swell on a run and toes hit the front on downhills. The heel should not lift as you walk, and nothing should press on the widest part of your foot. Buy in the afternoon, when feet are largest, and wear the socks you run in. If a shoe needs "breaking in", it is the wrong shoe — modern running shoes should feel right on the first run.

Wide Feet Have Real Options Now

If shoes pinch across the ball of your foot, going up a size is the wrong fix — it makes the shoe too long and your foot slides. Buy a wide fitting instead. The Ghost 17, Adrenaline GTS 25 and Bondi 9 all come in wide versions on Amazon; choose the width in the size selector on the listing.

When to Replace Them

Running shoe foam loses its bounce long before the upper looks worn. The usual guidance is somewhere between 300 and 500 miles, and the honest signal is that the shoe feels flat and your legs ache after runs that used to feel easy. If you run three times a week, that is roughly once a year. Keep an old pair for wet days and yard work, and do not race or do long runs in shoes that are finished.

Frequently Asked Questions

What running shoes should a beginner buy?

A neutral, moderately cushioned shoe that fits well - which means either of the budget picks here, or the Brooks Ghost 17 if you can stretch to it. Beginners do not need stability features unless they have a known problem, and do not need maximum cushion at low mileage. Fit and comfort on the first run matter far more than the model name.

How do I know if I need a stability shoe?

The honest answer is that most runners do not. Consider one if you have recurring shin, knee or arch pain that neutral shoes have not fixed, or if someone has watched you run and your ankles visibly collapse inward on landing. A running shop can film you on a treadmill in a few minutes. If you are comfortable and injury-free in a neutral shoe, stay in it.

Are expensive running shoes better?

Up to a point. The jump from a $75 shoe to a $150 daily trainer buys noticeably better foam that stays lively for more miles, and a more refined upper. The jump beyond that mostly buys specialisation - stability systems, extra stack height, racing plates - which helps specific runners and does nothing for others. The best shoe for you is the one that fits, not the one that costs most.

How often should I replace running shoes?

Typically every 300 to 500 miles. The foam compresses and loses its rebound before the upper shows much wear, so go by feel: if the shoe feels flat and your legs ache after easy runs, the cushioning is gone. Heavier runners and people who run mostly on concrete tend to reach the end sooner.

Can I use running shoes for the gym?

For cardio machines and light circuits, yes. For heavy lifting, no - a soft, high-cushion running shoe compresses unevenly under a loaded barbell and makes you less stable. The Brooks Revel 8 is the most versatile shoe here for mixed use. For serious lifting, see our cross-training shoes on the gym pages.
